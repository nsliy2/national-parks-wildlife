Protected Parks, Unprotected Wildlife?

Do Australia's national parks protect its wildlife, and which parks matter most?

By Nudara Liyanage · October 2026

More than a fifth of Australia's land is now protected, yet 700 of its animal species and subspecies are listed as threatened or extinct under national law. This page follows eight national parks, one in each state and territory, to ask a simple question: are the places we protect the places our threatened animals actually live?

1How much land is protected?

A protected area is land set aside mainly to conserve nature. Across Australia, 22.6% of the land is protected, but the share changes a lot depending on where you live.

The Australian Capital Territory protects more than half of its land and Tasmania more than two-fifths, while Queensland and New South Wales protect only about one hectare in ten. A large share of land protected does not tell us what kind of protection it is, though.

Not all protected land is a national park

Half of Australia's protected land is in Indigenous Protected Areas. National parks, the focus of this story, make up about a quarter.

Indigenous Protected Area
Land managed by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander groups under an agreement with the Australian Government.
National park
Public land run by government to protect nature, usually open to visitors.
Nature reserve
Public land with stricter protection, often with little or no public access.
Private land
Private property where the owner has signed a legal agreement to protect nature.

2Where are the national parks?

Australia has 797 national parks on the mainland and in Tasmania. They range from small islands of a few hectares to desert parks bigger than some countries.

The biggest parks sit in the dry centre and the tropical north, while the east coast is dotted with hundreds of small ones. Drag the slider to see how the park network has grown since the 1880s.

Queensland has the most national parks (320), but its typical park covers only about 3,400 hectares. The typical park in the Northern Territory is about 37 times larger. More parks does not mean more protected land.

3Which parks hold the most wildlife?

To see what lives in each featured park, this section uses the Atlas of Living Australia, an online database of animal and plant sightings recorded by scientists, government surveys and members of the public. A sighting shows where someone saw an animal, not how many animals live there.

Royal National Park, on the edge of Sydney, has 4,866 recorded animal species, about 13 times more than Kakadu. Part of this gap reflects how many people visit and record what they see, not only how much wildlife there is.

In six of the eight parks, insects make up the largest share of recorded species. Kakadu is the exception: 68% of its records are birds, which are easy to spot and popular with birdwatchers.

More species does not always mean more species at risk. The next chart ranks the parks twice: once by how many species were recorded, and once by the share of those species that are threatened.

The ranking flips. Kakadu has the fewest recorded species but the highest share under threat (15 of 331, or 4.5%). Royal has the most species but the lowest share (0.8%). A park does not need to be rich in species to matter for threatened animals.

4Are threatened animals protected?

Australia's national environment law, the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 (EPBC Act), lists species at risk in categories from "vulnerable" to "critically endangered", plus those already "extinct".

Birds and mammals have the most listed species. Mammals stand out for the worst reason: 39 mammals are already extinct, more than all other animal groups combined.

So where are threatened animals actually seen? The map below counts recent sightings of eight threatened animals, one linked to each featured park. Each square is half a degree of latitude and longitude; darker squares had more sightings. Choose an animal from the menu to zoom in on it.

Only 7% of all sightings fall within 50 km of the animal's featured park. The Southern Cassowary (26% near Daintree) and the Yellow-footed Rock-wallaby (23% near Ikara-Flinders Ranges) rely on their parks the most. Fewer than 1 in 50 Greater Glider and Gang-gang Cockatoo sightings are near their parks, and no recent Northern Quoll sightings were recorded near Kakadu.

What this means

National parks matter. They protect important strongholds, such as the Daintree for the Southern Cassowary, and remote parks like Kakadu hold a high share of threatened species. But national parks cover only a quarter of Australia's protected land, and most sightings of threatened animals happen outside them.

Protecting Australia's wildlife therefore depends on more than national parks. Indigenous Protected Areas, private conservation land, and the farms, forests and towns around parks all play a part.

A note on the data: sightings depend on where people look. Parks near cities, like Royal, are visited and recorded far more often than remote parks, so the true picture in remote areas may be different.
